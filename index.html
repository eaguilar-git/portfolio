The argument

For nearly two decades US electricity consumption was essentially flat. That era is over. EIA now puts average growth at 1.7% a year from 2020 through 2026, with commercial demand, the category that holds data centers, running at 2.6%.

A number that small sounds harmless. It isn't, because the grid it lands on was planned for the flat years. When load grows faster than the system can be built out, the adjustment has to show up somewhere: in interconnection queues, in who pays for new capacity, in where a factory or a data center decides to locate, and eventually in the price level.

Electricity is a macro variable. Power costs feed industrial competitiveness and inflation. Subsidies and state utility losses feed the fiscal balance. Fuel import dependence feeds the external accounts. Utility arrears become contingent liabilities, and contingent liabilities become debt.

That last chain is not hypothetical. In much of the world, the electricity sector is a fiscal problem: a state-owned utility that under-recovers its costs, a subsidy line that grows faster than revenue, arrears to generators that eventually land on the sovereign balance sheet. It's a standing item in debt sustainability work and in most IMF programs that touch energy. I've spent the last year inside exactly that problem in Honduras — losses, subsidies, utility solvency, supply adequacy — and the same year building financial models for transmission investment appraisal.

The awkward part is that the questions this raises sit between two disciplines that rarely share a desk. What a large buyer pays under a given tariff in a given interconnection zone is a microeconomic rate question. Whether a country's power costs are eroding its tradable sector, or whether its utility subsidies are consistent with a credible fiscal path, is a macroeconomic one. Same system, examined at different resolutions; the analysis usually stops at whichever end the analyst was trained on.

I trained on the macro end and specialized in the micro one. My master's was in international economics with quantitative methods: trade and tariff analysis, balance of payments, international financial relations and debt sustainability, applied econometrics. My work has been in power markets: cost-of-service, tariff design, PPAs, transmission economics, utility performance. I don't think of those as two careers. Reading a rate case and reading an external position are the same act of finding where a system's costs are actually being carried.

There's a second reason I think this vantage matters now. The advanced economies are entering a constrained-supply period that much of the world has never left. Systems I've worked in have spent decades managing exactly this: demand outpacing reliable supply, losses eating margin, subsidies straining budgets, investment decisions that can't wait for perfect information. The failure modes are known. They just haven't been read across.
